# Recovering a Failing Financial System Implementation

Client: Real estate investment arm of a large institutional client, managing a multi-billion-dollar commercial property portfolio.

Project: $6M vendor package implementation, migrating existing vendor financial accounting system to a new vendor solution.

My role: Senior Program Manager. Fourth PM was assigned to the project.

##The situation

Three senior project managers had already come and gone before I was brought in.

The client had chosen to implement their existing financial accounting system as customizations into the new vendor platform rather than adopt it as-is. That created a specific problem during validation: when a defect surfaced, no one could tell the origin: in the customization or in the base vendor product. Every defect became a dispute before it became a fix.

The project was four months behind schedule. The team was demoralized. The client’s executives were losing confidence in the vendor, and the relationship had escalated to the executive level, with legal action under discussion.

##Diagnosing the root cause

Early assessment revealed that the new vendor’s package had come into the company through an acquisition — the parent company had purchased it to build out a new division.

The maturity of the base product under new ownership became a risk. Combined with the client’s decision to heavily customize rather than adopt the platform as-is, two sources of risk were layered on top of each other — with no reliable way to isolate which one was responsible when something broke.

From prior vendor engagements, I knew which department controlled the outcome in a situation like this: product development, not sales or support. That assessment shaped the approach to the vendor relationship from the start.

##Rebuilding the team first

Before addressing the schedule or vendor relationship, the priority was the human factor: the team.

In our first meeting, a line was drawn down the center of a chalkboard. One side: what hadn’t gone well. The other: what had. Each team member used a marker and was asked to fill in both columns.

When they finished, the “went well” side was significantly longer.

The team had been focused on three failed PM transitions and a mounting defect backlog. They hadn’t stopped to see that they were doing more right than wrong. That exercise reset the tone for what followed.

##Fixing the accountability structure

A meeting was convened between the client’s executives and the vendor’s CEO and newly assigned director. In post meeting review, I recommended that the client work through the new director to remove the bottlenecks rather than continue through the existing escalation path.

The source of the slow turnaround became clear: defects were being routed to the vendor’s product development team, which was also building features for the next release. Defect fixes were competing with roadmap work — and losing.

Working directly with the new director, we changed the defect-resolution process.

Defect turnaround dropped to one day.

The vendor also increased the frequency of onsite consultant visits, putting more of their team in the room instead of working the queue remotely.

##Recovery

With the team reset and the defect bottleneck cleared, the project schedule was rebased against where the new and real implementation stood.

The first phase — an MVP — launched on the rebased timeline. The threat of legal action went away. I stayed through the recovery and MVP launch.

##What this shows

A failing implementation rarely fails for one reason.

The visible problem was a project which was four months behind schedule. But the schedule wasn’t the root cause. Underneath it was compounding product and customization risks, unclear defect ownership, a vendor relationship escalating toward legal action, and a team that had lost sight of its own progress.

The recovery started by addressing those conditions rather than pushing harder against the schedule.

The product and customization risks were identified. The team was realigned. The client-vendor relationship was reset. And the one-day defect turnaround created an accountability structure that allowed execution to move again.

##None of it was a scheduling fix. All of it made the schedule possible.

I call this Phase 0 — the discipline of assessing risk and establishing alignment, accountability, and governance before committing to forward execution.

Phase 0 doesn’t necessarily happen only at the beginning of a project.

Sometimes a program already in motion must return to those fundamentals when execution has outrun the conditions needed to support it. In this case, stepping back from the schedule long enough to understand the risk and perform discovery, realigning the people, and establishing clear accountability was what allowed the project to move forward again.
